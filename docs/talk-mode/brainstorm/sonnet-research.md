# Japanese speaking path: evidence + competitive teardown (Sonnet)

Scope: what builds real conversational speaking, what competitors do, Japanese/Korean-speaker pitfalls, legal data, steal/avoid. Verification tags: **[V]** = seen in a fetched/searched source this session; **[M]** = from my memory, not re-verified (check before relying on it).

Repo context read: README (FSRS, keyboard-first, real corpus sentences only, no AI-generated content, Tatoeba-keyed TTS clips for a speaking screen, no automatic speech scoring), design-prompt (Duolingo-style game layer, MCQ, character-first), docs/learning-evidence.md (retrieval + spacing, self-check, honest "not established" section). The existing evidence doc already cites Kim & Webb 2022 and Karpicke & Roediger; I build on those, not repeat them.

## 1. What works for speaking (and what is folklore)

Strength scale: **Strong** = meta-analysis/replicated experiments on the target construct. **Moderate** = consistent but small/short/lab studies. **Weak** = mixed or theory-led. **Folklore** = widely repeated, little direct support.

| Claim | Strength | Evidence | Design implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spaced practice beats massed for L2 learning | **Strong** (for vocab/grammar; speaking-specific data thin) | [Kim & Webb 2022, 48 experiments / 3,411 participants](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/C833408A4C3BAD939CA39EA734423BB7) [V]; effects differ by area, learner and activity. Repo doc already cites it. | Keep FSRS, but schedule *chunks* (sentences), not only characters. No study shows FSRS parameters tuned for oral production; treat as inference. |
| Retrieval (attempting recall) beats re-reading | **Strong** generally; **Moderate** for L2 speech | Karpicke & Roediger 2008 (already in repo doc). I found no study of spaced *spoken* retrieval of chunks specifically [V: search returned nothing on this]. | Make the card "say it before reveal", which the repo's self-check already half-does. Honest caveat: self-grade is unverified. |
| Practice must be skill-specific: knowing a rule does not give real-time fluency; practice must be *speaking under time pressure* | **Moderate-strong** (Skill Acquisition Theory; transfer-appropriate processing) | [DeKeyser & Criado 2013, Practice in L2 instruction](https://digitum.um.es/digitum/bitstream/10201/142082/1/DeKeyser%20and%20Criado_Practice%20in%20Second%20Language%20Instruction_2013.pdf) [V link]; summary in [Columbia SALT](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/view/1492). | MCQ recognition of a kanji reading will not by itself make anyone speak. Include timed production (prompt in Korean/English, answer in Japanese). |
| Explicit instruction of formulaic sequences raises fluency measures | **Moderate** (small experiments, mostly English L2; no meta-analysis found) | [Effects of focused instruction of formulaic sequences on fluent expression (CJAL)](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/CJAL/article/download/19898/21737/) [V]: +26.3% mean length of run, +13.8% speech rate after 6 weeks, small case study. [Can explicit instruction of formulaic sequences enhance L2 oral fluency? (Lingua)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0024384121000449) [V]: instruction group beat controls on pruned speech rate. | Core unit = chunk/formula (ありがとうございます, ちょっと〜, 〜んですけど), not isolated word. Reasonable bet, not proven for Japanese. |
| Shadowing improves pronunciation/prosody/fluency | **Moderate** for pronunciation; **Weak/mixed** for fluency proceduralization | [Systematic review of shadowing for L2 pronunciation, 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/29984475.2025.2546827) [V]: all 8 fluency studies reported positive results, gains clearer when programs exceed ~6-8 weeks and combine modeling + feedback [V per search summary]. Counter: Muraoka (2017) found shadowing alone did not promote fluency/proceduralization [V, via same search]. Also [Oxford review (Benen, Nadelek, Whitworth)](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:3104cae1-3a6b-400a-b173-de44384238d2/files/r1z40kv86w) [V link]. Many included studies are small, quasi-experimental, English L2. | Include shadowing, but as rhythm/prosody training with model + visible pitch contour + a comparison, not as "shadow 10 min and you're fluent". |
| Interaction with corrective feedback helps; negotiation of meaning helps | **Strong** for feedback (meta-analyses), **Moderate** for negotiation per se | [Mackey & Goo 2007: 28 interaction studies, d=0.71 immediate / 1.09 delayed](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1) [V via search summary; check which Cambridge item it is]. [Lyster & Saito 2010: 15 classroom studies, N=827, CF effects significant and durable; prompts > recasts](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/6w924g64b) [V]. Caveat: effect sizes inflated by lab-style tests that measure the practised item. | Feedback must be specific and prompt a retry ("say it again, long vowel") rather than silently give the answer. An app has no partner, so interaction loops need either an LLM partner or humans (tutor), see section 2. |
| Swain's output hypothesis (producing language forces noticing gaps) | **Weak-moderate**: mixed empirical results | Review snippet: [Testing the output hypothesis (SSLA)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/testing-the-output-hypothesis/8437C53035A5FD15BD985463E5428B36) [V link]; reviews report mixed results on whether modified output causally drives learning [V]. | Output matters mostly for automatization (above), less proven as a grammar-discovery engine. Do not claim "speaking makes you notice" as science. |
| Comprehensible input alone is sufficient (Krashen) | **Folklore as stated**; input is necessary, not sufficient for speaking | Critiques: i+1 never operationalised; [Frontiers 2025 critique](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12577063/) [V link]. Immersion programs show strong comprehension but weak productive accuracy (Swain's classic Canadian French immersion finding) [M]. | Heavy listening is required (the listening gap is real) but pair it with forced output. Avoid "just watch anime" framing. |
| Perceptual training (HVPT: many talkers, many tokens, feedback) improves perception, retains, generalizes to new talkers, sometimes to production | **Strong** for perception; **Moderate** for production transfer | [HVPT meta-analysis, 79 studies, medium-to-large effects, long-term retention, generalization (SSLA 2025)](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/6ABB8C1F32D88D53EA8D05A4565E76F6/S0272263125100879a.pdf/high-variability-phonetic-training-hvpt-a-meta-analysis-of-l2-perceptual-training-studies.pdf) [V]. Japanese-specific pitch-accent training: [Frontiers 2014 (musicians vs non-musicians)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01318/pdf), [PMC3472521](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3472521/) [V links]. | Minimal-pair/pitch drills using *multiple speakers* are the best-evidenced pronunciation feature we can build cheaply. Needs multi-voice TTS or CC0 recordings. Single-voice drilling is the weak version. |
| Fluency and accuracy trade off; speed first vs accuracy first | **Weak** (competing resource accounts, e.g. Skehan) [M] | Not verified here. | Offer two modes: fluency run (no corrections until end) and accuracy run. Do not claim one is optimal. |
| Learning styles, "think in the language from day one", "10,000 hours" style rules | **Folklore** | Standard debunks, not searched. | Avoid in copy. |
| "Pitch accent can wait" / "pitch accent is hopeless after childhood" | **Folklore both ways**. Perceptual training works in adults (above); mora-level rhythm errors hurt intelligibility more than pitch errors do [M] | Pitch errors usually reduce naturalness more than comprehensibility; pronunciation research generally weights comprehensibility [M]. | Teach moras/length first, pitch second with visual contours. |

Honest gap: I found no controlled study showing that a self-paced app, with spaced retrieval of spoken chunks, measurably improves *real-life conversation*. Everything above supports components. The repo's honesty style ("not established") should carry over to the speaking path.

## 3. Japanese conversation pitfalls to design around

**General (any learner):**
- **Register.** Textbooks teach です/ます; real daily speech is plain form + sentence-final particles. Teach both, tag each sentence with a register, and *train recognition of casual first*, production polite first (safer socially).
- **Aizuchi (back-channels: うん, はい, そうなんだ, へー, なるほど).** Absent from most apps; silence reads as non-comprehension. Give them their own drill category (hear a statement → pick/say an appropriate aizuchi).
- **Subject/topic omission.** Learners keep saying 私は/あなた. Cards should show natural sentences without pronouns; the translation layer supplies "(I)" in brackets.
- **Contractions and reductions.** ている→てる, ては→ちゃ, ければ→きゃ, のだ→んだ, じゃない, ちゃんと→ちゃんと. Drill *reduced forms as listening items*; learners trained on citation forms can't parse real speech.
- **Speech speed and the listening gap.** Real conversation is far faster than learner audio. Provide 0.75x/1.0x/1.25x playback and "native-speed" gating before a chunk is "done".
- **Kanji-to-reading burden.** Speaking path should be audio-first with *furigana on demand* (the repo's strength is kanji recognition, so show kanji with readings that matter for conversation, e.g. everyday on'yomi compounds).
- **Fillers and hedges** (えっと, あのう, ちょっと…, ですかね). Teach them as fluency tools; the evidence (§1) on formulaic sequences supports this.
- **Politeness asymmetry in requests/refusals.** 〜ちょっと難しいです as soft no. Pragmatics need role-play, not flashcards.
- **Counters, numbers, dates, irregular readings** (一日, 二十歳): high-frequency in daily scenes, error-prone.

**Korean-speaker advantages:**
- Near-isomorphic word order and agglutinative structure, particle system (は/が/を/に/で ↔ 은·는/이·가/을·를/에/에서), honorific system, and verb-final syntax: grammar load transfers.
- Huge Sino-Korean ↔ Japanese on'yomi overlap (this app's core premise). Many Korean 한자어 map to on'yomi with predictable sound correspondences (e.g. -ㄱ/-ㅂ endings ↔ -く/-つ/-ち; 학 ↔ がく; 국 ↔ こく; 법 ↔ ほう). A *sound-correspondence trainer* is a genuinely novel feature here [M, standard knowledge; I did not source].
- Similar sentence-final particle use (요, 죠, 네, 잖아 ↔ ですよ, でしょ, ね, じゃん).
- Honorific habits already internalized; keigo concept is familiar (though Japanese uses in-group/out-group (ウチ/ソト) humility more strictly than Korean's hierarchy-based use [M]).

**Korean-speaker pitfalls:**
- **Vowel length (長音: おばさん vs おばあさん).** Koreans lack contrastive vowel length in standard modern Korean; research shows Korean listeners judge any longer-than-usual vowel as long while Japanese listeners require ~2 morae of duration [V: [Tsukada, Interspeech 2020](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2020/tsukada20_interspeech.html) and related].
- **Geminate consonants (促音: きて/きって).** Singleton vs geminate stops are hard to perceive; Korean fortis consonants (ㄲ/ㅃ/ㄸ) can be mapped onto geminates, which helps in part but is incomplete [V: [Macquarie study](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/perception-of-japanese-consonant-length-by-native-speakers-of-kor/)].
- **Mora timing.** Korean is syllable-timed with closed syllables (받침); Japanese morae (ん, っ, ー) each get a beat. Learners compress or lengthen wrong units.
- **Pitch accent.** Korean (Seoul) has no lexical pitch accent, so accent patterns of words are new; research notes difficulty distinguishing accented vs accentless sets and interference from initial-consonant type [V: [Mie University paper on Korean learners' accent acquisition](https://mie-u.repo.nii.ac.jp/records/6616)]. Initial stops in Korean (lax/aspirated/tense) interfere with pitch onset in Japanese words. Teach with contour display.
- **し/じ/ち/つ/ず and ざ-line.** Korean has no /ts/ or /dz/ phoneme; つ→ちゅ/쓰 and ざ→자 substitutions; ず/づ→주/즈 confusions; も ふ/ひ (Korean ㅎ vs Japanese ふ [ɸ]); ら-line flap vs Korean ㄹ ok but ラ行 in word-initial can be mistaken as ㄴ/ㄷ. [M, standard contrastive-analysis knowledge; sound classes not sourced in this session].
- **Vowels う/え/お:** う unrounded [ɯ] is closer to Korean ㅡ than 우, so a Korean learner who maps う→우 over-rounds; え/お vs 에·애/오·어 mapping errors [M].
- **Devoicing** (です→"des", ます→"mas", き/し/ち before voiceless) and **word-medial voicing of ㄱ/ㄷ/ㅂ**: Koreans over-voice or over-aspirate initial stops (か/た/ぱ). [M]
- **は/が vs 은는/이가.** Looks equivalent, mostly is, but diverges on contrastive は, wa-ga in embedded clauses, and が/は in questions, plus は topic vs が focus in noun-modifying clauses [M, standard]. Also false-friend particle uses: に vs 에 for existence/place vs 에서/で, で vs 으로, ~に会う (Korean takes 을/를 or 와/과) [M].
- **False friends in 한자어:** same kanji, different meaning (e.g. 大丈夫, 勉強, 先生, 約束 stay close; 愛人, 工夫, 結構, 汽車, 手紙 diverge) [M, standard list; the repo's compare card is already positioned for this].

## 5. Steal / avoid (10)

1. **Steal: audio-first, answer-aloud-before-reveal cards (Pimsleur pause, Glossika L1→L2).** Best match to the retrieval and skill-specificity evidence; reuses the existing self-check UI.
2. **Steal: chunks and formulas as the unit, not words/kanji.** Formulaic-sequence instruction has the most direct (though small-study) fluency evidence; Japanese daily talk is highly formulaic.
3. **Steal: mistake-capture from human tutor sessions into the SRS (iTalki loop).** Fixes tutors' biggest weakness (lost feedback) and makes the app complement the highest-evidence interaction rather than compete with it.
4. **Steal: pitch contour shown on the learner's own sentence (Suzuki-kun/OJAD style), with multi-speaker minimal-pair drills.** HVPT is the strongest-evidenced pronunciation method; this is the niche no consumer app owns.
5. **Steal: Korean-L1 prompt layer plus 한자어↔on'yomi sound-correspondence trainer.** Unique to this owner and to the repo's core premise; no competitor offers it.
6. **Steal: casual-first listening, polite-first speaking, with register tags and an aizuchi category.** Fixes the textbook-register gap that most products ignore.
7. **Avoid: XP/streak/combo animation as the headline reward for speaking.** Evidence doc already says not to judge by XP; for speaking, headline metric should be minutes spoken aloud and later-day first-attempt recall, LingQ-style hours rather than points.
8. **Avoid: automatic pronunciation "scores" from generic ASR.** Speak users report recognition that silently corrects errors [V]; a score implies validity the evidence doesn't support. Use waveform/pitch overlay and self-compare, state the limit in the UI like the existing doc does.
9. **Avoid: AI-generated free-form dialogue as the core engine.** Unverifiable correctness, conflicts with the repo's "real corpus only" rule, and Speak-type tools already crowd it. If used, label it optional practice and keep scheduled content human-authored/corpus-based.
10. **Avoid: shipping CEJC/BTSJ/NHK Easy/Irodori/JSUT-audio/Forvo content.** Licences are non-commercial/educational-only or unknown [V]; build on Tatoeba (CC BY), Common Voice (CC0), JMdict (CC BY-SA), plus commissioned original dialogues; use restricted corpora only for statistics.
